# Programme-Model Precedents — Small/Bootstrapped Companies Only

**Researched 2026-08-24.** Scope: does "sell hardware outright + bundle a FINITE structured
programme (not a perpetual subscription)" have a working precedent at **small or
resource-constrained** scale — the regime this company will actually launch in? A parallel
thread covers large VC-funded comparables; this file is deliberately restricted to
companies that were small, bootstrapped, or under-capitalised, plus non-fitness analogues
of the same "pay once, finite programme, defined endpoint" structure.

**Headline answer: yes, but the strongest precedent is not a fitness-hardware company at
all — it is the healthcare-employer sector's shift toward *outcome-based, per-episode*
pricing (Sword Health), and the oldest, best-tested version of "pay upfront for a
bounded-duration result" is orthodontics. Among running-hardware peers, the only company
that has survived a decade doing anything like our model is Stryd, and it survived by
selling hardware outright with subscription strictly optional — not by bundling a finite
programme. Nobody in this sample has run "outright sale + mandatory finite programme,
then done" at small scale long enough to prove it works. The closest fitness-hardware
attempt at bundling outright sale with a structured, bounded thing (RunScribe, clinics) is
also the one company that had to abandon the consumer market entirely to survive.**

**Important correction to this project's own prior research:** `market-teardown.md` and
`unit-economics.md` cite **Playermaker** as "the proven structure in this exact segment" for
outright-sale-plus-programme. That framing needs qualification — see §6. Playermaker has
raised **$45M+ in VC funding** (Wikipedia, 2023 figure). It is not a small or bootstrapped
comparable; it belongs in the parallel VC-funded research thread, not this one.

## 1. Fitness/Running Hardware — Small-Scale Comparables

### Stryd — outright sale, subscription optional, a decade of survival
**The only running-hardware company in this file that has survived roughly a decade doing
something adjacent to our model — but it did NOT bundle a finite programme.**

- **Funding: ~$1.05M total** — a $300K seed (Aug 2015), a $225K round (Dec 2016), Kickstarter
  proceeds, and an NSF grant. **Effectively bootstrapped** by hardware-startup standards.
  [Crunchbase](https://www.crunchbase.com/organization/stryd/company_financials) ·
  [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/stryd/__7A6PVbRS8qZdpN93PVg1Aj90ESfg8J76D3bm6yRUEek/funding-and-investors)
- **Kickstarter, March 2015: exceeded the $50,000 goal in about four hours**, over $80,000 on
  day one. Pre-sales funded working capital before committing to inventory.
  [BizWest](https://bizwest.com/2015/03/03/stryd-launches-power-meter-for-runners-via-kickstarter/)
- **Business model: the pod works fully without a subscription.** A membership layer —
  **$14.99/month or $129/year**, adding training plans, race planning, advanced analytics —
  was bolted on in 2021 as a strictly optional upsell, not a requirement to use the core
  product. [DC Rainmaker, 2021 subscription launch](https://www.dcrainmaker.com/2021/04/launches-subscription-platform.html)
- **Scale estimate (2026):** independent analysis triangulating app-store ratings, Garmin
  Connect IQ downloads, Strava club membership and social following puts lifetime pod sales
  at **400,000–700,000 units (500,000 midpoint)**, with **~150,000–300,000 active users** but
  only an estimated **~10,000 paying subscribers (~2% take rate)**, or roughly **$1.3M/year**
  in subscription revenue. The analyst's own read: the company is sustained mainly by
  **hardware sales**, not the subscription layer.
  [the5krunner, July 2026](https://the5krunner.com/2026/07/03/stryd-users-subscribers-estimate/)
- **Why this is not a clean precedent for us:** Stryd never built a bounded "programme."
  There is no graduation event — a customer owns the pod indefinitely and either subscribes
  or doesn't. It validates "outright sale can sustain a small running-hardware company for a
  decade" but says nothing about whether a *time-boxed programme* specifically helps or hurts
  that.

### RunScribe — tried consumer outright sale, had to retreat to B2B to survive
**The most directly relevant small-company data point, and a cautionary one.**

- Founded 2014 (Tim Clark, John Litschert), San Francisco, **unfunded** — no VC raised at all.
  [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/runscribe/__2Web_lp0nmD2Q0Fq8i04RjjWOGD8XSgJ2MUlK-yxbqA)
- **Original model: consumer outright sale, ~$249 for a two-pod bundle.**
- **2017–2019: the company shrank its team and pivoted away from direct-to-consumer toward
  clinics, gait labs, and universities.** Founder Tim Clark's own explanation: *"The
  economics only work at scale, and that's just not where we are right now."* The consumer
  tier was **discontinued**, not supplemented — pricing for the professional line roughly
  **doubled** versus the old consumer bundle.
  [DC Rainmaker, Feb 2019](https://www.dcrainmaker.com/2019/02/runscribe-consumer-launches.html)
- **Current state (checked 2026-08-24, runscribe.com):** RunScribe now explicitly markets
  itself as **"a professional grade gait analysis platform … designed for experienced gait
  professionals"** — coaches, podiatrists, physical therapists, gait labs. It sells an
  optional **"RunScribe PRO"** subscription (video courses, custom branding, multi-client
  management) on top of hardware. As of this check, the main **RunScribe Red is out of stock
  due to tariff issues**, with a research-focused "Motion POD" on pre-order and older stock
  being liquidated. A small company, still alive, but no longer serving individual consumers
  at all.
- **The lesson for us:** a small team selling running-gait hardware outright to individual
  consumers could not make the unit economics work *even with no VC burn to service* — and
  the fix was to abandon the consumer channel, not to add a bundled programme. This is a
  warning specifically against assuming "no subscription treadmill" solves small-company
  economics by itself; CAC and support cost per consumer unit were the actual problem, not
  the billing model.

### ARION / ATO-Gear — real-time coaching insoles, survived small, model is hybrid freemium
Already logged in this project's `market-teardown.md` as "19 employees after 11 years" —
confirmed via Tracxn (19 employees as of April 2026, founded 2015, Eindhoven, two funding
rounds from 4 investors, latest round undisclosed amount June 2023 — Series A stage, not a
large raise). [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/arion/__dwgbnT_nOZqnhXLiK7uNj3F_8VQzc5WanfYnMXqku5w)

- **Pricing: hardware sold outright, €249 (no GPS) / €299 (with GPS)**, per a runner-gear
  retailer listing. [runreborn.com](https://runreborn.com/article/10-off-arion-wearable)
- **Business model, per ATO-Gear's own EU Innovation Council project description:** *"initially
  based on hardware sales enhanced by disposables and SaaS. The accumulating installed user
  base is valorised via B2C freemium and B2B SaaS models. A professional coaching platform
  and access to new training programmes form the basis of a freemium model."*
  [EU EIPP project page](https://ec.europa.eu/eipp/desktop/es/projects/project-9636.html)
- **Reading between the lines:** this is "outright hardware + optional ongoing coaching
  layer," structurally similar to Stryd, not a bounded finite-programme model. No public
  source states a fixed programme length or an explicit "graduation" moment. ARION is real
  survival evidence for *narrow, single-sport, small-team, real-time-coaching hardware* — but
  it is evidence for the freemium-hardware pattern, not specifically for our finite-programme
  pattern.

### Lumo Run / Lumo Bodytech — failed, but the public record does not isolate the pricing model as the cause
- Company (Lumo Bodytech, formerly LUMOback) raised **$16.3M total**, made posture-coaching
  wearables including Lumo Run (single waist-worn running-form pod). **Ceased operations;
  assets acquired by Seismic.** [CBInsights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/lumo-bodytech)
- A Stanford GSB case study exists (*"Lumo Bodytech: A Bumpy Journey"*, 2018) but is gated to
  Stanford/Case Centre access; the public abstract discusses team-formation, product-pivot,
  and financing-structure (venture debt vs. equity) struggles rather than pricing model
  specifics. [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/case-studies/lumo-bodytech-bumpy-journey)
- **Honest limitation:** despite searching, no public source ties Lumo Run's failure
  specifically to "subscription vs. outright sale" as the mechanism. `market-teardown.md`
  already logs Lumo Run under "form-coaching as a standalone product has a poor track record
  even at one sensor" — the deeper cause looks like product-category (single-metric form
  coaching, no clear behaviour-change loop) rather than billing structure. Do not cite Lumo
  as evidence against or for the finite-programme model; the sourced evidence isn't there.

### Sensoria — never a mainstream consumer product; the retreat predates any "programme" question
- **2013 Indiegogo campaign, Heapsylon (later Sensoria):** raised the funding target with
  smart socks, **MSRP set at $199** for the two-sock-plus-anklet consumer bundle.
  [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/07/31/smart-socks/) ·
  [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2013/06/20/555508/10037131/en/Video-Release-Heapsylon-Launches-Crowdfunding-Campaign-for-Wearable-Technology-Sensoria-Fitness-Smart-Socks-for-Runners.html)
- The consumer running-sock product never scaled; the company is now **Sensoria Health**, a
  B2B clinical/RPM (remote patient monitoring) platform selling into rehab and home-health
  settings (USC Keck, UCLA, Baylor, Shepherd Center). It bills through **CMS RPM CPT codes
  99453/99454/99457/99458** — i.e., **providers bill insurance per monitoring period**, not
  a consumer subscription or a one-time consumer sale at all.
  [Sensoria Health](https://www.sensoriahealth.com/) ·
  [ODT Magazine](https://www.odtmag.com/exclusives/smart-socks-monitor-gait-improve-balance-and-track-rehab/)
- **Current pricing to individual patients/clinics is not published** — Sensoria's own
  solutions pages route all pricing questions to a sales contact, consistent with a B2B/RPM
  billing model rather than a disclosed retail price.
- **Lesson:** this is the second running-adjacent hardware company in this file (after
  RunScribe) that **abandoned direct-to-consumer entirely** and moved to a clinical/insurance
  billing model to survive. Two-for-two on that pattern among the small companies researched
  here is a meaningful signal, independent of subscription-vs-outright debates.

## 2. NURVV — Isolating the Business-Model Question From the Product-Quality Question

This project's existing files already treat NURVV as the closest failed analogue (16-sensor
insoles, real-time coaching, $9M+ Series A, insolvent August 2023) and blame the app/coaching
quality (`market-teardown.md`). The user's question here is narrower: **did the pricing
model itself also contribute, independent of software quality?**

- **Confirmed: NURVV's pricing was outright sale, no subscription.** One-time hardware
  purchase at **$299** (insoles + GPS pods + app), with **no ongoing subscription or app fee**
  identified anywhere in the DC Rainmaker in-depth review or subsequent coverage. Replacement
  insoles (consumables, ~1 year life at 30mi/50km-per-week use) were sold separately at
  **$80**. [DC Rainmaker in-depth review](https://www.dcrainmaker.com/2021/02/nurvv-depth-review.html)
- **Funding: $9M Series A (Feb 2020, led by Hiro Capital)**; some secondary sources cite
  cumulative raises up to ~$34M, though this could not be independently confirmed from primary
  sources in this pass. [tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2020/02/07/nurvv-series-a/)
- **Conclusion: NURVV's business model was already the model this project is proposing** —
  outright hardware sale, no subscription treadmill. **It still failed.** That is actually
  useful evidence in our favour on one narrow point: *outright sale did not cause NURVV's
  failure* — the causes this project has already documented (coaching that contradicted
  running science, an app that contradicted its own metrics, incoherent training plans, a
  $299 price for metrics a cheaper watch already gave) are all independent of the billing
  structure. **NURVV is evidence that "outright sale" is necessary but nowhere near
  sufficient** — it does not protect you from a bad coaching product. It also offers no
  evidence either way on whether *bundling a finite structured programme* (as opposed to
  indefinite unstructured hardware ownership, which is what NURVV actually shipped) would
  have changed the outcome.

## 3. Non-Fitness Analogues — "Pay Once, Finite Programme, Defined Endpoint"

### Orthodontics / Invisalign — the oldest, most-tested version of this pattern
- **Pricing: $2,500–$8,000+, typically paid upfront or via a financing plan arranged by the
  practice — but the practice itself is paid in full upfront even when the patient pays over
  time via BNPL or in-house installments.** The practice does not carry recurring-revenue
  risk on an individual patient; a lender or the patient's own payment plan does.
  [Cherry — Invisalign payment plans](https://withcherry.com/blog/invisalign-payment-plan) ·
  [Parsons Ortho](https://www.parsonsortho.com/news/invisalign-payment-plan)
- **This is structurally the strongest available precedent for "pay once (or on a fixed
  schedule) for a bounded-duration outcome, then you are done."** Treatment has a clear
  defined endpoint (final aligner, retention phase begins), which is the same "graduation"
  psychology this project is relying on for its 8-session program.
- **Limitation found in this research pass:** no public, sourced data surfaced directly
  comparing completion/adherence rates *by payment structure* (upfront vs. financed vs.
  hypothetical subscription) within orthodontics. The industry-wide practice of upfront
  full payment (regardless of how the patient personally finances it) is well documented;
  a causal claim that upfront payment specifically drives completion is not something this
  search substantiated with a citable number. Treat the "high completion because it's paid
  upfront and finite" logic as plausible and consistent with practice norms, not as a
  proven causal finding.

### CPAP + compliance coaching — hardware + program bundling is billed on *compliance*, not time
- **Federal/payer structure directly rewards a finite, outcome-gated model:** during the
  first three months of a CPAP rental, **the supplier is paid only if the patient hits a 70%
  usage-compliance threshold**; if the patient fails, the supplier must halt billing and can
  be required to refund. [Sleep Review — CPAP adherence guide](https://sleepreviewmag.com/sleep-diagnostics/home-testing/cpap-data/cpap-adherence-solutions-guide/)
- **"Sleep Coach" programs are a paid add-on layer DME suppliers use specifically to hit that
  compliance bar** — a proprietary outreach platform assigns a coach per patient to drive
  usage during the qualifying window, explicitly to protect revenue that is otherwise at
  risk under the compliance-gated billing rule.
  [Medtrade](https://medtrade.com/news/respiratory-sleep/increase-cpap-compliance-resupply-orders-and-revenue-with-sleep-coaches/)
- **ResMed's U-Sleep** (a similar compliance-management/coaching layer) is reported to
  produce a **59% reduction in staff labor** associated with coaching CPAP patients — i.e.,
  software-assisted structured coaching measurably reduces the cost of hitting a compliance
  gate. [ResMed U-Sleep](https://www.resmed.com/en-us/health-professionals/solutions/u-sleep/)
- **Relevance to us:** this is the clearest real-world precedent for **payment gated to
  programme completion/outcome rather than to elapsed time** — directly analogous to
  "graduation" as the unit of value, not "months retained." It is a B2B/payer model (DME
  supplier ↔ insurer), not consumer-direct, but the mechanism — pay for a defined outcome,
  not a subscription — is exactly our thesis, and it is proven at meaningful scale in a
  regulated healthcare market.

### Sword Health — the strongest structural analogue found, though not "small" or fitness
Not a small company (well-funded digital health unicorn; acquired Kaia Health for $285M in
Jan 2026), but the **billing structure itself is the single closest real-world instance of
"finite structured programme paid by outcome, not perpetual time" found in this whole
research pass**, so it is included for the mechanism, not the company size.

- **September 2024: Sword Health moved from engagement-based pricing to "Outcome Pricing."**
  Under the old model, the employer/payer paid based on member engagement thresholds. Under
  Outcome Pricing, **"clients don't pay in full until the program achieves agreed-upon
  results, regardless of the number of sessions required."**
  [Contrary Research — Sword Health business breakdown](https://research.contrary.com/company/sword-health)
- **Reported figures (2024):** 81% member adherence; 62% pain-free; 64% moved to
  surgery-low-risk; ~$3,000 saved per engaged patient/year; **100% client (employer)
  retention** at 10/10 satisfaction.
- **Why this matters for us even though Sword is large:** it is direct market evidence that
  **a "you don't pay until the programme finishes and works" structure is commercially
  viable and preferred by payers over open-ended subscription billing** in a structurally
  similar category (a device-assisted, coached, physical rehabilitation programme with a
  real clinical endpoint). Hinge Health has moved a similar direction — an **upfront platform
  fee plus per-session/engagement charges** rather than flat perpetual subscription.
  [Sacra — Hinge Health](https://sacra.com/c/hinge-health/)
- **Caveat:** this is B2B2C (employer pays, not the patient), which changes the sales motion
  entirely from our direct-to-consumer plan. The *pricing mechanism* transfers as a concept;
  the *go-to-market* does not.

### Noom — the clearest published data that a finite programme keeps producing results after it ends
- **Largest-ever Noom RCT (reported June 2026):** members who completed Noom's **16-week
  programme kept losing weight for the following 52 weeks**, reaching **4.1% average body
  weight loss at week 68** — vs. much smaller loss in controls. **41% of Noom participants
  reached ≥5% body-weight loss at 68 weeks vs. 19.5% of controls.**
  [Noom press release, June 2026](https://www.noom.com/in-the-news/noom-members-kept-losing-weight-a-full-year-after-the-program-ended-largest-ever-noom-randomized-clinical-trial-shows/)
- **42% of users who lost ≥10% body weight maintained that loss at 2 years post-programme**
  (same release).
- **Retention caveat:** the 86.7%-retained figure quoted in coverage is *study retention*
  (participants who stayed in the trial for measurement), **not subscription renewal** —
  do not conflate the two. Noom's actual commercial product today is a recurring
  subscription with an internal "16-week programme" framing, not a true one-time purchase —
  so this is evidence for the *psychological/behavioural* claim ("a framed, bounded
  programme produces durable post-programme results") rather than evidence about billing
  structure per se.
- **Relevance to us:** this is the best available outside evidence that our core mechanism —
  **finite, structured, faded-feedback intervention produces effects that outlast the
  programme itself** — is a real, replicated pattern in a comparable behaviour-change
  category, not an assumption unique to the gait-retraining literature already cited in
  `DECISIONS.md`.

### WeightWatchers/WW — a cautionary contrast, not a supporting data point
- **WW's core model is perpetual subscription, not a finite programme**, and it is under
  visible strain: **behavioural (non-GLP-1) subscribers fell 25% YoY to 2.5M by Q1 2026**;
  a **45% churn rate** was reported specifically among members forced through a **billing-model
  transition**; and only **~20% of a large compounded-semaglutide cohort were retained**
  through a subsequent disruption. [Investing.com — WW Q1 2026 earnings transcript](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-ww-internationals-q1-2026-shows-strategic-shift-amid-revenue-decline-93CH-4668521)
- WW does have one deliberately finite, graduation-like feature — **"Lifetime Member"**
  status, granted once a member hits and holds their goal weight for 6 months, after which
  they get free/low-cost ongoing membership — an internal acknowledgment that a defined
  finish line is worth building into an otherwise-perpetual product.
- **Relevance to us:** WW is the negative case — perpetual-subscription behaviour-change is
  visibly leaking members, and the one part of the model it kept as "graduation" is treated
  as a reward, not routine. This supports the graduation framing indirectly, by showing what
  the alternative (no finish line) currently looks like at a company that has tried it for
  decades.

### GolfTEC — service-sector analogue combining device-based diagnosis with a bounded lesson package
- **Model: paid Swing Evaluation ($95–$149) using proprietary 3D motion-capture (OPTIMOTION),
  followed by fixed-length Lesson Plans sold in 3, 6, or 12-month packages** (not perpetual
  membership by default), each with a defined number of lessons and practice sessions. An
  entry package bundles a swing evaluation, club fitting, 2 lessons, and 2 practice sessions
  for **$395**. [GolfTEC pricing](https://www.golftec.com/golf-lesson/plans-pricing) ·
  [GolfTEC swing evaluation](https://www.golftec.com/swing-evaluation)
- GolfTEC has been operating since 1995 (200+ centers across the US/Canada at its peak) —
  a genuine multi-decade survival case for "device-assisted diagnosis + a bounded, packaged
  improvement plan," even though the device stays in-store rather than going home with the
  customer (closer to our VALD comparison in `market-teardown.md` than to owned hardware).
- **Relevance to us:** the closest sports-technique precedent to "diagnose with hardware, then
  sell a fixed-length improvement package," but it is a **service/franchise business**, not a
  hardware company — customers do not keep the sensor. It validates the *packaging* (bounded
  lesson tiers rather than perpetual membership) more than it validates *owned* hardware
  economics.

### Not found: a clean small-company hardware analogue outside running/soccer
Despite targeted searching, no additional small, bootstrapped, non-running/non-soccer
hardware company selling "device once + bundled finite improvement programme" with public
financial or outcome data surfaced in this pass (checked: tennis-form sensors, additional
golf wearables like Zepp/Arccos — both are freemium hardware-plus-app, not bounded
programmes; "Moveo Health" as named in the research brief could not be identified as a
distinct company matching this pattern — search results returned unrelated physical therapy
clinics of similar name, not a hardware/programme company). This gap itself is informative:
**the "owned hardware + mandatory bounded programme, then done" structure is rare enough in
the wild that this research pass could not find a second clean example beyond the fitness
category**, which raises the degree of execution risk this project is taking on by being
one of the few to try it as the *primary* model rather than an optional add-on.

## 5. Sourced Answer To The Two Direct Questions

**Q1: Does "outright sale + finite programme" have ANY precedent of working at
small/bootstrapped scale?**

Partial precedent only, and it requires stitching together two different pieces that no
single company demonstrates together:
- **"Outright sale can sustain a small hardware company for a decade" — yes, proven by
  Stryd** (bootstrapped, ~$1.05M raised, survived ~10 years) **and ARION** (19 employees
  after 11 years, Series-A-only funding). Neither bundles a *finite, bounded* programme —
  both are indefinite hardware ownership with an optional ongoing coaching/subscription
  layer.
- **"A finite, bounded structured programme produces durable results and is worth paying for
  as a discrete event" — yes, proven by Noom's RCT (behavioural, software-only) and by the
  entire orthodontics industry's payment norms (upfront-for-bounded-outcome), and increasingly
  validated commercially by Sword Health's Outcome Pricing** — but none of these are small
  hardware companies, and none are running/fitness-hardware specifically.
- **No company in this research pass has combined both** — small, bootstrapped, sells owned
  hardware once, bundles a genuinely time-boxed programme with an explicit end/graduation —
  long enough to generate public outcome data. This project's plan would be a novel
  combination of two separately-proven pieces, not a replication of an existing proven whole.

**Q2: What's the strongest non-fitness analogue?**

**Sword Health's Outcome Pricing (payment gated to programme completion/result, not
elapsed subscription time) is the strongest *mechanism* match** — it is the only real
commercial billing structure found that pays specifically for the "graduation" event rather
than for tenure. **Orthodontics is the strongest *psychological/behavioural* match** — the
oldest, most broadly normalized instance of consumers paying upfront for a bounded-duration
outcome with a defined finish line, though the causal completion-rate data this project
would want does not appear to be publicly sourced. If forced to pick one: **Sword Health**,
because it is the only analogue with disclosed, recent, quantified outcome and retention data
tied directly to a finite/outcome-gated payment structure rather than to general industry
norms.

## 6. Correction Flagged For DECISIONS.md / market-teardown.md / unit-economics.md

Both `market-teardown.md` (§2, §3) and `unit-economics.md` (§3, §7) currently cite
**Playermaker** as "the proven structure in this exact segment" for outright-sale-plus-
programme, used to justify the $249/$299 pricing decision. This research pass found:

- **Playermaker has raised $45M+ in venture funding** (Wikipedia cites "$45 million by 2023";
  other secondary sources — SGB Media, Tracxn — report figures in the **$40–60M** range across
  investors including Ryan Sports Ventures, Ventura Capital Group, MIG Capital, Pegasus Tech
  Ventures, and Arsène Wenger personally).
  [Wikipedia — Playermaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playermaker) ·
  [SGB Media — $40M raise](https://sgbonline.com/playermaker-raised-40-million-in-funding-round/)
- **This means Playermaker is not a small/bootstrapped comparable.** It belongs in the
  parallel VC-funded research thread. Its $249-with-one-year-subscription pricing and its
  50+ D1 college / 100+ club distribution remain real and worth citing for **go-to-market**
  (per-player, parent-funded, sold through the club) — but it should **not** be cited as
  evidence that a small/resource-constrained team can replicate that pricing-and-distribution
  outcome, since Playermaker had $45M+ of capital to build the sales motion, certifications
  (FIFA EPTS), and brand partnership (Manchester City "CityPlay") that likely drove that
  reach.
- **No public renewal-rate or repeat-purchase data was found for Playermaker** despite
  targeted searching — the $149/year auto-renewal mechanism is documented, but the *actual
  renewal rate* is not publicly disclosed anywhere located in this pass.
- **Recommended action:** update `market-teardown.md` §2 and `unit-economics.md` §3/§7 to
  note Playermaker's funding level explicitly wherever it is used as a proof point, so a
  future session doesn't read "proven structure" as "proven at our resource level."
