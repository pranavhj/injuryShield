# Demand Side — Does Anyone Actually Want This?

**Researched 2026-08-24.** Every prior research round asked whether the product *can* work.
This one asks whether anyone *wants* it and will pay.

**Headline: three findings are favourable, one is a serious problem.**
1. ✅ Runners have the money — ~$1,000/year, with $328–729 on coaching alone
2. ✅ Runners already believe biomechanics causes injury — problem awareness exists
3. ⚠️ But the culture says "only change form if injured" — narrows the buying trigger
4. 🚨 **A direct competitor is already shipping at our exact price point, and a free app
   layer attacks from below.** We are squeezed on both sides.

---

## 1. Willingness To Pay — Favourable

Recreational runners spend **$937/year (men) to $1,132/year (women)**, ~21% higher for women.

| Category | Annual spend |
|---|---|
| Shoes | ~$103 per pair, multiple pairs/year |
| Watch / tracker | ~$126 average; GPS watches $200–600 |
| **Races** | **~$270** |
| **Coaching** | **$328 (men) – $729 (women)** |
| Apparel | Significant; women spend 40–60% more |

**The coaching line is the important one.** Runners already pay $328–729/year for *guidance
on how to run* — which is exactly what we sell, delivered by software instead of a person.
That is a real, existing budget line and the closest substitute for our product.

**A $249 one-time purchase is ~25% of a runner's annual spend and less than a year of
coaching.** There is headroom. This is not a market that lacks money.

---

## 2. Problem Awareness — Favourable, With An Irony

A JOSPT study of what recreational runners believe causes injuries found:

- **Runners strongly believe in the importance of footwear and biomechanics** as injury
  causes
- Nearly three-quarters believed stretching and appropriate shoes prevent injury
- Excessive training was also named
- **But the researchers explicitly note a gap: "current evidence does not robustly support
  these as major injury risk factors"**

**Commercially this is good news.** We do not have to educate the market that form matters —
they already believe it. Problem awareness is the hardest thing to buy and it exists for free.

**Intellectually it is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly.** Runners believe
biomechanics predicts injury. Our own evidence review (`predictive-validity.md`) concluded it
does *not*. What actually works is retraining — which changes mechanics and reduces injury
through a mechanism nobody has established.

So we would be selling into a belief that is not well-founded, with a product that works for
adjacent reasons. **That is ethically fine — the intervention has RCT support — but we must
never reinforce the false belief to make the sale.** The claim discipline in
`buyer-and-liability.md` §2 already forbids it. This is a second reason to hold that line.

---

## 3. Cultural Resistance — Real, And It Narrows The Trigger

There is an established, coach-led position in running against changing form:

> *"The human running stride is a self-optimising system that advances automatically toward
> maximum efficiency through simple repetition."*
> *"Clients run the way they do because that's how their nervous system learned it, and
> so many times when they set out to change it, they get hurt."*
> *"Willing yourself to run differently than what comes naturally… makes you less efficient
> and may even lead to injury."*

**But note where the consensus actually lands:**

> *"Runners should change their stride only to avoid injury risk, not to improve
> performance."*
> *"If you run fast and never get injured, then why mess with it? But if you're injured a
> lot… it may be worth it."*

**That is our positioning, almost verbatim.** We do not claim performance benefit — the
ARION RCT found none (3.07% PB change, p=.26). We claim injury reduction, which is the one
reason the running establishment endorses form change.

**The cost is that it narrows the buying trigger.** The market's own rule is *change form
when injured or injury-prone*. Nobody buys this on a random Tuesday. Likely triggers:
- Just got injured, or coming back from one
- Repeatedly injured, wants to break the cycle
- Starting out and wants to do it right
- Building for a goal race and worried about breaking down

**Tension with our own evidence:** Chan's RCT enrolled *novice, uninjured* runners and
prevented injuries. The market's belief is "only change if already hurt." Our best evidence
supports the preventive case; the culture supports the reactive one. **Selling prevention
to healthy runners is fighting the culture. Selling to the recently-injured is easier but
is a smaller, reactive market.** This is a positioning decision that needs deciding.

---

## 4. 🚨 The Competitive Squeeze — The Serious Problem

`market-teardown.md` covered hardware companies and missed two adjacent layers entirely.
Both attack the consumer product directly.

### Layer 1 — Phone-camera gait apps, at near-zero price

| App | Price | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ochy** | **~$5/mo or ~$59/year** | 4.6★, AI gait analysis, **no wearable — phone camera only**, results in under 60s |
| Movaia | — | Direct Ochy competitor |
| GaitLab, StrideCoach, Running Pose | — | Multiple iOS entrants |

Ochy's pitch: *"record running form with your smartphone and receive detailed analysis in
under 60 seconds — no extra devices required."* Slow-motion, rear-view footage is reportedly
up to 4× more precise than normal speed.

**This is a real floor on pricing.** A runner asking "should I pay $249 for form analysis"
now has a $59/year answer with zero hardware and zero setup friction. Our advantages over it
are genuine — continuous in-run data, per-limb tibial acceleration, real-time cues, no
filming setup — but they must be *explained*, and explaining is expensive.

### Layer 2 — 🚨 Aletheia Run: a direct competitor, already shipping

Launched ~December 2025. This is the closest thing to our product that exists.

| Attribute | Aletheia Run |
|---|---|
| Hardware | Small sensor **strapped to the waistband at the sacrum** |
| Metrics | **Symmetry across all three planes**, braking impulse, impact, sway, hip mobility/stability L&R, cadence, flight time, contact time |
| Feedback | **"Real-Time Tracking — monitor your metrics live during every run"** |
| **Price** | **$239/year, sensor + charger + belt INCLUDED, cancel anytime, 30-day free trial** |
| Platform | iPhone only, Android "coming soon" |
| Founder | A physical therapist who owns a running performance centre |
| Extras | Full lab service — 3D motion capture, pressure mapping, treadmill testing |

**What this means:**

1. **The subscription model we rejected is being run in the market right now.** $239/year,
   hardware included, no lock-in. Our calculation says they need ~9 months of average
   customer life to break even. **We should watch whether they survive it** — it is a live
   experiment on our own rejected hypothesis, and it costs us nothing.
2. **They took the sacrum; we would take the tibia.** That is our genuine differentiation.
   A sacrum sensor cannot measure **peak tibial acceleration** — the variable Chan's RCT
   actually used. They measure impact *proxies*; we would measure the thing itself.
   That distinction is defensible but subtle, and subtle is hard to sell.
3. **They combine device + clinic** — the A′ option from `VIABILITY.md`, already executed.
4. **They make the claim we decided we cannot make.** Their site asserts *"70% of injuries
   stem from poor biomechanics"* and that they *"catch problems before they sideline you."*
   Our evidence review says that is unsupported. They also cite *"79% of runners sustain an
   injury annually"* against the literature's 37–56%.

**That last point is the uncomfortable one.** They are making a stronger, more marketable
claim than the evidence supports. We decided not to. **Either that honesty is a
differentiator that earns trust, or it is a handicap in a market that wants to be told
biomechanics predicts injury.** We should assume it costs us conversion and price that in.
It also means they carry a liability exposure we do not — see `buyer-and-liability.md` §2.

### Where that leaves our pricing

| Option available to a runner | Cost |
|---|---|
| Phone-camera app (Ochy) | **$59/year** |
| Aletheia Run, hardware included | **$239/year, $0 upfront** |
| **Us — 2-pod kit + 6-week programme** | **$249 one-time** |
| Clinic gait analysis, per session | $75–500 (US), £72–98 (UK) |

We are not badly priced — **$249 once beats $239/year from year two onward.** But we have
**$249 of upfront friction against their $0**, and we are 4× the app layer.

## 5. What Is Still Unknown — Needs Interviews, Not Search

Desk research cannot answer these. They are the core of P8.4.

1. **What actually triggers a purchase?** Injury, race build, new-runner anxiety, a friend?
2. **Would they pay $249 upfront rather than $239/year?** Upfront friction vs total cost.
3. **Do they believe a device over a coach or physio?**
4. **REPEAT PURCHASE.** Still the biggest unknown. One programme per customer is a small
   business. What would bring them back?
5. **Does "we won't tell you your injury risk" read as honest or as useless?** This directly
   tests whether our claim discipline is an asset or a handicap.
6. **Novice vs experienced.** Chan's evidence is in *novices*; the money and gear appetite
   are in *experienced* runners. **We may have evidence for one segment and revenue in the
   other.** This is the sharpest open question in the file.

---

## 6. Honest Verdict

**Demand-side is more favourable than expected on money and awareness, and less favourable
than expected on competition.**

Favourable: the money exists ($1,000/year, $328–729 on coaching), problem awareness is free,
and the establishment's own rule — change form to avoid injury, not for performance — is our
exact positioning.

Unfavourable: **we are no longer early.** Aletheia Run is shipping our category at $239/year
with $0 upfront, bundled with a clinic, making a bolder claim. A $59/year app layer sets the
floor. The window where "nobody has productised real-time running biomechanics" was true has
closed, and `crux-analysis.md`'s founding premise needs updating to reflect that.

**This does not kill the project.** Competition validates the market, Aletheia's sacrum
placement leaves the tibia genuinely open, and their subscription model may fail on the
economics we already calculated. But it does mean **differentiation now has to be argued
explicitly, and the honest-claims position has to be a deliberate wedge rather than an
accident.**

**It also raises the cost of being wrong about repeat purchase.** Against a subscription
competitor, a one-time sale needs either a much lower price, a much better product, or a
repeat trigger. We have not established any of the three.
