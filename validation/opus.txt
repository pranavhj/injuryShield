I did a deep pass on this. Here's what the record actually shows, then my honest read.

The short version

The specific product you described — a clip-on IMU pod on the shoe giving runners actionable insight during and after a run — is not an untried idea. It's one of the most thoroughly attempted and most consistently fatal product categories in consumer fitness hardware. Roughly a dozen well-funded teams have run this exact play since 2013. One survived at meaningful scale. Your instinct that "most companies were not able to sell this correctly" is right about the outcome but I think wrong about the cause: it wasn't a go-to-market failure, it was a value failure that go-to-market couldn't paper over.

Why they died — the five mechanisms

1. The actionability gap. This is the core one. The best evidence in the field is a 2024 RCT in the American Journal of Sports Medicine using instrumented insoles with real-time feedback on 220 runners. Intention-to-treat showed no difference in injury rate (HR 1.11); the as-treated analysis was positive (HR 0.53), but 33% of participants ended up unassigned because they used incorrect wearable settings. Read that twice: in a supervised university trial, a third of users misconfigured the device. Your "setup friction" worry isn't a UX polish item, it's the thing that nulls the primary endpoint. 
nih

2. Platform absorption. Garmin discontinued the Running Dynamics Pod because a growing number of its watches deliver Running Dynamics straight from the wrist. And the price the market puts on embedded sensing: Under Armour sold connected HOVR shoes for only $10 more than the non-connected model, while Nike and Adidas dabbled and stepped away from connected footwear. Your product sits in the strike zone of a free watch feature.

3. Hardware economics. FCC certification with a pre-certified BLE module runs $3,000–$10,000, total certification budget realistically $20,000+, and getting a product designed, molded, tested and certified ranges from ~$50,000 into the millions. Then you own returns, warranty, battery degradation, and a cloud bill forever — Nurvv's users learned what happens when that bill stops being paid.

4. Retention. Roughly a third of activity-tracker users stop within six months. A run-only accessory has worse odds than a wrist device, because it isn't passive.

5. Nobody is the buyer. Runners already own a watch, a phone, and Strava. The insight has to beat "increase your cadence a bit," which is free.

The clinical path, honestly

This is the part I'd most want you to update on. Hinge Health, the biggest company in digital MSK, deliberately deleted the wearable sensors. Their S-1 states the 2023 strategic decision to shift away from kits with tablets and wearable sensors, replacing all member sensors with AI-powered motion tracking. The result: gross margin went from 66% in 2023 to 77% in 2024, reaching ~81% by Q1 2025. Sword Health's acquisition of Kaia is read the same way — recognition of the friction inherent in shipping hardware kits.

The reimbursement math also doesn't rescue it. Remote Therapeutic Monitoring pays roughly $40 per 30-day cycle for MSK device supply and ~$54 for the first 20 minutes of management time — about $94/month for an engaged patient, and that money goes to the clinician, who must do the 20 minutes. Your 7-sensor full-body plan

Set this aside. IMU joint-angle estimation error is typically 5° to 18.8° depending on joint and plane, against a ~5° threshold for clinical gait applications, and that's before sensor-to-segment misalignment, magnetometer disturbance indoors, and a user placing seven pods on themselves. "Judging how well an athlete is doing" from 7 body-worn IMUs is a research programme, not a v1.

What I'd actually do, in order
Build the insight engine before any hardware. A phone in a running belt has a 9-axis IMU at 100Hz+. If your coaching layer isn't valuable running off a phone strapped to someone's waist, a custom pod won't save it. This costs you nothing but weekends.
Prove the counterfactual on 20 runners. Not "is the data accurate" — everyone's data is accurate. Ask: did they change something, did it stick past week 4, would they pay $10/month. If you can't clear that with a phone, stop.
Find a buyer with a budget and a workflow, not a consumer. RunScribe's 1,500 clinics and Playermaker's youth-academy pivot both point the same way: someone who already charges for assessments and needs a report.

Three good pushbacks. Taking them in order.

You're right that "phone in a vest" is not a product

I should have been clearer: the phone isn't a v1, it's a measurement rig. Its only job is to answer "does an insight exist here" before you spend $50K+ on tooling and certification. A watch won't serve that purpose because you can't put it where the interesting signal is, and you can't get raw high-rate IMU out of it easily. The phone gives you 100–800Hz accel + gyro, on-device compute, and a build-test loop you can run every evening.

But your instinct exposes a real hole in what I said. A phone at the waist measures the pelvis — and that's roughly the location whose data is already commoditized. Which brings us to the actual question.

What watches already give you

Apple Watch has measured vertical oscillation, stride length and ground contact time from the wrist since watchOS 9, plus running power. Garmin delivers the same set natively on newer watches, which is why they discontinued the Running Dynamics Pod. So: cadence, stride length, GCT, vertical oscillation, vertical ratio, power, plus load/training-status modeling — all free, already on the wrist, already synced.

If your product's output is any of those numbers, you have nothing. That's the honest read.

What they genuinely can't give

There are four signals a wrist device structurally cannot produce, all of which require a distal mount:

True bilateral asymmetry. A watch produces one aggregated stream. Garmin's GCT balance is an estimate derived from a single torso sensor. Two foot pods measure left and right independently — contact time, impact magnitude, flight time, per side. This is the gap RunScribe built its entire clinical business on.
Foot strike angle, pronation velocity, eversion excursion. Distal-only. No wrist model gets there.
Peak tibial/shoe acceleration — impact load per limb. Not a form metric, a dose metric. Cumulative per-limb loading across a session and across a week, which is what bone stress injury actually tracks with.
Per-limb degradation under fatigue. How asymmetry opens up in the last 20% of a run, per side.

So yes, there's real data behind the wall. The harder question is your second one.

Is any of it actionable in a way that isn't already covered?

Split it honestly:

Form change — weak. "Reduce your pronation velocity" requires sustained motor learning, has thin evidence for injury reduction, and is exactly what the leading reviewers say about Stryd's gait metrics: compelling, hard to act on. The 2024 insoles RCT is the ceiling of this evidence, and it was null on intention-to-treat.

Load and asymmetry — strong, and different in kind. The actionable insight is not "change how you run," it's "you're 14% asymmetric at week 3 of return-to-run, don't progress volume this week." That works because:

The action is do less / progress slower, which requires no motor learning and near-zero adherence effort.
There's a person making a dated decision — a PT clearing someone for return-to-run, or progressing an ACL/Achilles/stress-fracture patient — who currently makes it on eyeballs and patient self-report.
Nobody's watch does it, and nobody's watch will, because it needs two sensors on the feet.

That's your wedge if there is one: objective per-limb load for return-to-run, sold to whoever owns that decision. Not "improve your running form," which is the graveyard.

Practical note: the phone-at-waist test validates your insight layer and report, not this differentiated sensing. For the bilateral piece, two off-the-shelf BLE IMU logger boards taped to shoes will cost you a couple hundred dollars and get you a 20-person pilot. Still not a hardware program. And given you've already built pose estimation in Python, a phone-camera treadmill capture is a third zero-hardware path to the same report — that's the direction Hinge went.

Ask or tell? Neither, in that form

Don't ask what to build — people invent features they'll never pay for. Don't lead with "I have this" — they'll be polite and you'll learn nothing.

Ask about the past, show an artifact.

Who to talk to, in your area: sports PT clinics that already advertise running gait analysis, podiatrists doing running assessments, collegiate athletic trainers, and coaches with paid rosters. The filter that matters: they must already charge for an assessment or already own a tool (video software, force plates, an instrumented treadmill). If they've never paid for a tool, they never will.

What to ask, all backward-looking:

Walk me through the last runner you cleared to return to running. What did you base it on?
What did you write in the report? Can I see one with the name removed?
When were you last unsure and cleared them anyway?
What's the last piece of tech you bought for the clinic? Do you still use it? What made you stop?
Who signs off on a $2,000 purchase here?

Then the "tell," but as an artifact rather than a pitch: run one of their patients on your rig, produce the one-page report, hand it over, and ask whether they'd pay you $75 to do the next five. Selling the assessment as a service before any product exists is the only test that isn't self-flattering. If ten clinics won't buy five assessments at $75, the sensor was never the problem.

Great physics, terrible moat — anyone who solves one gets the rest nearly free, and two companies already have a head start. Sorry for pointing you at it without checking.

Your rig idea: the physics gives you a clean answer

Stereo depth error scales with distance squared divided by baseline. A compact rig has maybe 20–40cm of baseline. At 20 metres, that gives you depth error far too large to adjudicate anything. This is exactly why Hawk-Eye uses 6–12 cameras spread around the ground with precisely measured positions, and why professional badminton Hawk-Eye does the same.

So the rule is simple and it decides your product:

A compact rig is excellent for events that happen near it, and useless for events far from it.

Now map your own list of disputed decisions onto that:

Decision	Where the event happens	Verdict
Run-out / stumping	At the crease	Solvable. Pure observation, high fps, near-field
Wide	At the batter's end, against a painted line	Solvable. Geometry, not prediction
Nick / caught behind	At the bat	Partly. Audio + frame timing, false-positive prone
LBW	20m of flight + a predicted path	Not solvable by any rig you can build

Notice that three of your four most-disputed calls are in the solvable column.

On umpire-worn specifically: don't. A body-worn camera has unknown, constantly changing extrinsics — the umpire sways, ducks, turns, and the bowler runs through the frame. You'd have to re-estimate camera pose every frame from crease and stump landmarks, which is doable (it's the PnP work you've already done) but it injects error exactly where you need precision. A cheap tripod is strictly better than a human tripod.

On stump-mounted: mostly no. Stumps get struck, bails fly, they're pulled at each innings, clubs use cheap sets — and a camera on the stumps can't see the stumps, which are your geometric reference.

The placement that actually wins is square leg. Side-on to the popping crease is the ideal view for run-outs and stumpings, and at amateur level that position is frequently unmanned. One tripod, one high-fps camera, one job: the square-leg umpire that never blinks.

The two problems you flagged as hard — one is unsolvable, one isn't a camera problem

Fingers under the ball on a catch: genuinely unsolvable from outside. This is occlusion, not resolution. The fingers you need to see are behind the ball by definition. More fps and more pixels don't help. The only physical routes are sensing inside the ball (contact/pressure — the direction MCC's chipped ball is going) or from the ground. Leave it alone; broadcasters with unlimited budgets haven't cracked it either.

Pro ball tracking being unreliable isn't a hardware failure, it's an epistemic one. The post-impact path is extrapolated from a model fitted to the observed flight. Error grows with how far the ball must be projected and shrinks with how much flight you saw — which is why full deliveries and impacts close to the stumps are the worst cases. "Umpire's call" exists precisely to encode that uncertainty band. No camera count removes it.

The strategic consequence matters more than the technical one: if you ship LBW, you inherit an argument you cannot win. The pros can't, with six figures of kit and a professional operator. Your product would get blamed for uncertainty that is inherent to the problem.

Is cricket gold? Honestly: good problem, hard business

What's genuinely good:

Dispute density is high and the calls people fight over are, by your own account, mostly the tractable ones.
No incumbent owns the solvable subset. Fulltrack and MCC are both aiming at the glamour call.
The setup complexity — ends changing every over, no fixed court, variable grounds — is annoying, but it's also the moat. It's the reason In/Out and SwingVision can't extend into cricket the way they extended into padel. Friction that repels generalists is an asset if you're willing to eat it.

What's genuinely hard, and I don't want to soften it:

Geography splits volume from money. India has the amateur base; UK and Australian club leagues have budgets and a documented umpire shortage. Your biggest market and your paying market are different countries.
Half the room doesn't want your product. Every wrong decision has a beneficiary. Adoption isn't a sales problem, it's a governance problem — the league has to mandate it, the way Tennis Australia and the LTA legitimised SwingVision.
No-balls are a trap, and your own observation is why. You're right that many are bowled and few called. Automate it and you'll suddenly call three times as many, change match outcomes, and get thrown out of the league. Build it, keep it off by default, ship it only when a league asks.
Match logistics are brutal — 3 to 8 hours, no power, outdoor light changes, and equipment that has to move ends every six balls.
You'll be judged on the call you can't do. Every conversation will start with "does it do LBW?" You need a crisp answer ready.

Net: I'd rate cricket clearly above running, gym and badminton — the first idea in this conversation where the physics, the dispute, and the absence of an incumbent all line up. But the product is not "amateur DRS." It's a narrower thing: objective adjudication of run-outs, stumpings and wides, sold to leagues rather than players.

The test, this weekend

One phone at square leg, 240fps slow-mo, five club matches. Two questions:

Can you adjudicate every run-out and stumping from that footage alone, with nothing else?
How many such decisions occur per match, and how many were disputed?

If the answer to (1) is yes and (2) gives you more than a couple per match, you have something real and you'll have proven it for the cost of a tripod. Then add wides, which need only the wide line and the ball's crossing point.

And keep the negative results — "we don't do LBW, and here's the physics of why nobody at this price can" is a more credible pitch to a league secretary than a promise you'd have to walk back.

None died because the technology didn't work. Motivation was the binding constraint in the only case where we got to see inside. You have that in surplus for cricket specifically. That's not a small thing to bring to a hard problem.

What genuinely stops mattering: BOM cost and 3–5x markups, the India-volume-versus-UK-budget split, league procurement cycles, whether anyone will pay $150/year. All of that was me pricing a venture outcome. Delete it.

What still bites, even for free:

Accuracy asymmetry doesn't care about your business model. A wrong machine call is worse than no machine, because now the argument is about your tool and you've made someone's Saturday worse. This is the one place I'd keep the discipline: ship the calls you can adjudicate cleanly, and say plainly what you don't do. Being trusted on run-outs beats being argued about on LBWs.

Free software with real users still has a support burden. Nurvv's customers got bricked when the servers went dark. If a league starts depending on you, you've taken on an obligation. Design for it now — everything works offline, on-device, no account required, no server that can die.

And swap the test rather than dropping it. The money question was only ever a proxy for "is this real." Replace it with: did someone use it in a real match, unprompted, twice? Second use with no nudge from you is the whole signal.

On the pathways — you're right, and specifically:

Labeled amateur cricket footage barely exists. A tool people actually run in matches generates a dataset nobody has, and datasets are where the leverage compounds. The same pipeline that adjudicates a run-out also gives you auto-scoring, highlight clips and coaching feedback — the adjacent products build themselves off the same base. And it's a real credential in the field you've wanted to work in, which is worth more than most side projects ever return.

So: one tripod, square leg, 240fps, five matches. See if you can call every run-out and stumping from that footage alone.

That's a weekend, and if it works you'll know something almost nobody else does.
